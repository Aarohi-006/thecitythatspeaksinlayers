01 · LOWER MANHATTAN

TRIBECA

Quieter and cleaner: fewer stickers, so each one stands out more.

The name comes from the "Triangle Below Canal Street." Old warehouses here became expensive lofts, and the streets are kept tidy. Stickers don't last long, so the few you find feel like small acts of rebellion: one slap on a mailbox, a tag on a pole that hasn't been scraped yet.

02 · LOWER MANHATTAN

SOHO

Where street art meets shopping. Artist stickers sit next to brand ads on the same lamppost.

"South of Houston." In the 1960s and 70s, artists moved into the empty cast-iron buildings and turned them into studios and galleries. Today the galleries are mostly stores, but the poles on Broadway, Prince and Spring Street still carry the art, layered over by logos and pop-up ads.

03 · WEST VILLAGE

W 4TH STREET

Around Washington Square Park and the basketball courts, student flyers and small art stickers fill every pole.

This is Greenwich Village: the neighborhood of the 1960s folk scene, jazz clubs and NYU. The fenced courts at West 4th draw crowds, and the poles nearby are covered with handmade stickers, show flyers and small drawings, many of them by students.

04 · EAST VILLAGE

ST. MARKS PLACE

The loudest poles in the city: punk, band and skate stickers layered so thick the metal disappears.

St. Marks Place was the heart of the East Village punk scene in the 1970s and 80s, full of record stores, tattoo shops and cheap food. The stickers still carry that energy. Band names, skate brands and protest slogans pile up on top of each other, and nobody scrapes them off.

05 · BROOKLYN

WILLIAMSBURG

Artists' neighborhood. Stickers, slaps and murals on almost every corner around Bedford Avenue.

In the 1990s and 2000s, artists moved across the river into old factories and warehouses in Williamsburg. Street art followed. Around Bedford Avenue and the waterfront, whole walls are murals, and every pole, door and pipe has stickers from artists who live just around the corner.
