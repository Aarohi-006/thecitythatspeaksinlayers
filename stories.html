STICK IT TO THE CITY

CHAPTER 01 · 01

FROM LABEL TO MESSAGE

IT BECAME SOMETHING THAT COULD IDENTIFY A PERSON, IDEA, OR PLACE.

01

Stickers are closely connected to the history of adhesive labels. Early labels were used for practical purposes such as identifying goods, displaying prices, and providing information.

The history of the modern sticker is not attributed to one single invention. In 1839, Sir Rowland Hill introduced the adhesive postage stamp in Britain. In 1935, R. Stanton Avery developed commercially successful self-adhesive labels, helping establish the peel-and-stick format used today.

As printing and adhesive technologies developed, stickers became easier to produce and distribute. Their purpose expanded beyond labeling and advertising into branding, art, politics, identity, and personal expression.

The sticker became more than something that identified an object.

CHAPTER 01 · 02

DIFFERENT SURFACES, DIFFERENT STORIES

THE CITY IS NOT THE BACKGROUND.
IT IS PART OF THE STICKER.

02

A sticker never exists on a blank canvas.

In New York, stickers appear on dustbins, poles, walls, pillars, cars, mailboxes, construction barriers, and storefronts. Each surface changes how the sticker is seen.

A sticker on a car can travel through different neighborhoods.

A sticker on a pole becomes part of a dense field of signs, notices, and other stickers.

A sticker on a dustbin becomes part of everyday infrastructure.

A sticker on a wall can become one element within a larger collection of marks.

A sticker on a pillar can accumulate with others over time.

The surface affects the sticker through its texture, shape, scale, movement, and visibility.

CHAPTER 01 · 03

SMALL FORMAT. LOUD INTENTIONS.

THE DESIGN IS SMALL.
THE CONTEXT IS THE CITY.

03

A sticker is a small format for graphic communication.

Within a limited area, it can combine typography, illustration, color, symbols, imagery, and text.

Its small scale can make the message immediate. A name can function as a signature. A symbol can identify a group. A short phrase can communicate an idea in seconds.

But the designer controls only the sticker itself.

Once it enters the street, everything around it becomes part of the visual context: other stickers, graffiti, advertisements, dirt, cracks, signs, and architecture.

CHAPTER 01 · 04

WHO GETS TO SPEAK?

WHO GETS TO BE SEEN?
WHO GETS TO SPEAK?

04

New York's streets already contain many forms of communication.

Official signs provide information. Businesses advertise products. Institutions post notices. Artists and individuals use posters, graffiti, stickers, and other forms of street expression.

Stickers offer a small and accessible format for placing a message into this environment.

That message can be commercial, humorous, political, personal, artistic, or anonymous.

Because stickers occupy existing public-facing surfaces, they become part of a larger question about visibility and access to public space.

NO SPACE IS SILENT

CHAPTER 02 · 01

MANY VOICES, ONE SURFACE

ONE SURFACE CAN HOLD MANY VOICES.

01

A sticker rarely appears alone.

On a single pole or wall, stickers can sit beside posters, graffiti, advertisements, signs, and other stickers.

Each mark carries its own message, but together they create a larger visual field.

Some messages are official. Some are commercial. Some are personal. Some are anonymous.

They may compete for attention, sit beside one another, or overlap until their original boundaries become difficult to separate.

CHAPTER 02 · 02

WHO OWNS THE WALL?

PUBLICLY SEEN DOES NOT MEAN PUBLICLY OWNED.

02

A surface can look public without being available for anyone to use.

A storefront may belong to a business. A wall may belong to a property owner. A utility pole is part of public infrastructure. A car belongs to an individual.

When someone places a sticker onto one of these surfaces, the sticker becomes visible to the public while physically occupying something that may belong to someone else.

This creates a tension between expression, visibility, and ownership.

The sticker may communicate freely, but its physical location still has a context.

CHAPTER 02 · 03

ONE MESSAGE BECOMES A CROWD

ONE MESSAGE BECOMES A CROWD.

03

One sticker is an individual mark.

Hundreds of stickers become something else.

As stickers accumulate, their individual messages begin to interact through color, scale, typography, repetition, overlap, and placement.

A surface can become dense enough that no single sticker dominates.

The collection becomes the image.

What began as separate acts of communication becomes a shared visual environment.

CHAPTER 03 · 01

EVERY STICKER HAS AN EXPIRATION DATE

ITS TEMPORARY NATURE IS PART OF ITS LIFE.

01

Stickers are temporary objects.

Rain can weaken the adhesive. Sunlight can fade colors. Repeated contact can damage the surface. People can peel stickers away, paint over them, or cover them with something new.

Because of this, the appearance of a sticker changes after it enters the city.

Its edges curl.

Its colors fade.

Its surface becomes dirty.

Its message can eventually disappear.

CHAPTER 03 · 02

THE SECOND LIFE OF A STICKER

THE STICKER BECOMES A TRACE OF ITS OWN HISTORY.

02

A sticker changes after it leaves the person who made it.

It can be torn, folded, scratched, written over, or partially covered by another sticker.

The original image may become difficult to recognize.

But traces remain.

A corner of paper.

A layer of adhesive.

A faded color.

A fragment of text.

These remnants show that something was once there.

CHAPTER 03 · 03

THE CITY REMEMBERS IN PIECES

A TORN STICKER CAN TELL US THAT ANOTHER STICKER EXISTED.

03

A sticker-covered surface can contain evidence of different moments.

Something was placed.

Something was covered.

Something was removed.

Something new appeared.

The surface becomes a visual record—not a permanent archive, but a temporary one.

What remains may be incomplete, but even a fragment can reveal that another image or message existed before it.

CHAPTER 03 · 04

I STARTED LOOKING DIFFERENTLY

I WASN'T JUST WALKING THROUGH THE CITY.
I WAS READING IT.

04

Before this project, a pole was a pole.

A mailbox was a mailbox.

A wall was a wall.

Once I started looking for stickers, these ordinary surfaces became something else.

I began noticing typography, symbols, color, repetition, placement, scale, damage, and layers.

Walking became a form of collecting.

Looking became a form of reading.

The city started to feel less like a finished image and more like something constantly being written and rewritten.
